It is easy to spend months adding features before anyone uses a product. A smaller first release gives you something concrete to put in front of the people you want to help.

An MVP—a minimum viable product—is a useful first version built to test an important assumption. It should help a specific group complete a meaningful task. It is not a reason to ignore basic reliability or the information people trust you with.

## Talk to people before choosing the features

You can learn about the problem before the product is live. Ask potential users how they handle the task today, where they lose time, and what they have already tried.

For example, if you want to build a booking tool, find out how bookings are received, confirmed, changed, and followed up. A polished calendar may be less useful than fixing the confirmation process.

[Y Combinator’s guide to planning an MVP](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6f-how-to-plan-an-mvp) describes a sequence of talking to users, launching a focused version, and collecting feedback. The first release supports that learning rather than completing a long feature list.

## Choose one clear outcome

Write a simple statement: “This version lets this type of user complete this task.” Use it to decide what belongs in the first release.

For a service enquiry tool, the first useful workflow might be receiving an enquiry, assigning it to someone, and recording the next action. A custom dashboard theme and a large reporting suite can wait until the basic workflow has proved useful.

Put deferred ideas in a separate list. Keeping an idea out of the first release does not mean rejecting it forever.

## Keep the first version small, not careless

The first version still needs the foundations appropriate to its job: sensible access controls, protected configuration, usable forms, and a way to recover important data.

If the product accepts payments or private records, those features need deliberate design. You can simplify the scope around them, but should not assume a prototype is ready to handle real customers simply because it runs.

Choose a technical approach you can maintain. Manual work behind the scenes may be reasonable for an early workflow when it is clear, controlled, and appropriate for the users.

## Launch to a group you can learn from

A small, relevant group can give more useful feedback than a broad announcement. Invite people who have the problem you are trying to solve and help them get started.

Watch the journey: where do they pause, what do they misunderstand, and which steps need explanation? A person who says the idea is interesting has given different evidence from someone who repeatedly uses it to complete real work.

## Record behaviour alongside feedback

Pick a few signals related to the product’s purpose. Depending on the workflow, they might include completed bookings, time to first useful action, repeat use, or enquiries successfully followed up.

Discuss the result with users. If someone stopped using the product, ask what changed and how they solved the task instead. Do not treat every request as a feature you must immediately build.

## Decide on the next useful change

Group feedback by the problem it reveals. Fix a confusing core step before expanding the product into several new directions.

A simple review can ask: what did people try, what worked, what failed, and what is the smallest change that could improve the outcome? Repeat that cycle as the evidence becomes clearer.
